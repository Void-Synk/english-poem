01 · Meet the poet

William Wordsworth

1770–1850 · English Romantic poet

Wordsworth was one of the leading poets of the English Romantic movement. His poetry often explores the beauty of nature, ordinary experiences, memory, emotion, and the relationship between people and the world around them.

He believed that close attention to the natural world could deepen human feeling and understanding. In this poem, he applies that sensitivity to a city: London appears almost as peaceful and beautiful as a natural landscape.

Literary context

Romantic poetry values personal feeling, imagination, nature, and the wonder found in everyday sights.

02 · Before you read

Setting, form & central idea

The setting

Westminster Bridge in London, early in the morning on 3 September 1802. The city is unusually quiet, and the view stretches across buildings, river, fields, and sky.

Form

A Petrarchan sonnet: 14 lines, traditionally organised into an octave (8 lines) and a sestet (6 lines). The poem uses a formal, musical structure to express a moment of wonder.

Central idea

The speaker feels that the view of London at daybreak is exceptionally beautiful. The city seems calm, bright, and harmonious because it is free from the usual movement and noise.

The poem asks us to pause and notice beauty where we might not expect to find it.
03 · Study section 1 · Lines 1–4
01

Beauty beyond comparison

Earth has not anything to show more fair: Dull would he be of soul who could pass by A sight so touching in its majesty: This City now doth, like a garment, wear

Vocabulary · this section

FairBeautiful; pleasing to look at.
Dull of soulInsensible or unable to appreciate beauty.
TouchingDeeply moving; stirring emotion.
MajestyGreatness, dignity, or impressive beauty.
DothAn old-fashioned form of “does”.
GarmentA piece of clothing; here, a metaphorical covering.

Meaning in simple English

The speaker says that Earth has nothing more beautiful to show. Anyone who could walk past this sight without feeling moved would be emotionally insensitive. London is now wearing beauty like a piece of clothing.

Explanation

The opening is full of admiration. The city is personified as if it can dress itself in beauty. “Like a garment” suggests that the morning light seems to cover and transform London.

Notice the technique

Simile: the city wears beauty “like a garment”. Personification: the city is presented as if it can wear clothing.

04 · Study section 2 · Lines 5–8
02

A city open to the sky

The beauty of the morning; silent, bare, Ships, towers, domes, theatres and temples lie Open unto the fields, and to the sky; All bright and glittering in the smokeless air.

Vocabulary · this section

SilentQuiet; without sound.
BareUncovered or unobstructed; here, clearly visible.
DomesRounded roofs on buildings.
TemplesBuildings used for worship.
UntoAn old-fashioned word meaning “to” or “towards”.
GlitteringShining with reflected light.
SmokelessFree from smoke or haze.

Meaning in simple English

In the still morning, the city’s ships and buildings are clearly visible. They seem to open out towards the fields and sky, shining brightly in clean air without smoke.

Explanation

The list of ships, towers, domes, theatres, and temples shows the variety of London’s skyline. “Silent, bare” does not mean the city is empty forever; it describes how peaceful and unobstructed it looks at this hour.

Notice the technique

Visual imagery: the reader imagines buildings shining in the clear morning air. The list of structures creates a broad panorama.

05 · Study section 3 · Lines 9–11
03

Light and deep calm

Never did sun more beautifully steep In his first splendour, valley, rock, or hill; Ne'er saw I, never felt, a calm so deep!

Vocabulary · this section

SteepTo cover or bathe thoroughly in something; here, sunlight.
SplendourMagnificent beauty or brilliance.
Ne’erPoetic contraction of “never”.
ValleyLow land between hills or mountains.
CalmPeacefulness; absence of disturbance.

Meaning in simple English

The speaker says that the rising sun has never lit up a valley, rock, or hill more beautifully. He has never seen or felt such profound peace before.

Explanation

Wordsworth usually associates beautiful scenes with nature. Here, the morning light makes London feel just as magnificent as natural landscapes. The exclamation expresses how strongly the stillness affects him.

Notice the technique

Comparison: London is measured against valleys, rocks, and hills. Sensory imagery: the calm is not only seen but also felt.

06 · Study section 4 · Lines 12–14
04

The sleeping city

The river glideth at his own sweet will: Dear God! the very houses seem asleep; And all that mighty heart is lying still!

Vocabulary · this section

GlidethAn old-fashioned form of “glides”; moves smoothly.
Sweet willIts own natural choice or unhurried course.
VeryUsed for emphasis: the houses themselves.
MightyPowerful, great, or enormous.
HeartHere, a metaphor for the busy centre or life of London.
Lying stillRemaining motionless and quiet.

Meaning in simple English

The River Thames flows smoothly and freely. Even the houses appear to be asleep. The great, powerful city—the “mighty heart”—is temporarily still.

Explanation

The river’s movement contrasts with the stillness of the buildings. Calling the city a “mighty heart” recognises London as a powerful, busy place, but at this moment it rests like a living being.

Notice the technique

Personification: the houses seem asleep. Metaphor: London is a “mighty heart”. Contrast: the flowing river and the still city.

07 · Literary appreciation

How the poem creates its effect

✦

Imagery

Bright buildings, clean air, the river, and the open sky help readers picture the early-morning scene.

≈

Personification

The city wears beauty, houses seem asleep, and London becomes a “mighty heart”.

◇

Simile & metaphor

Beauty is like a garment; the city’s life is imagined as a heart that has become still.

☼

Contrast

The normally busy city is quiet; the river moves while buildings appear motionless.

♫

Tone

Reverent, peaceful, amazed, and deeply appreciative.

◎

Theme

Beauty can appear in unexpected places when we slow down and pay attention.

08 · Revision & discussion

Check your understanding

Key points to remember

  • The poet is William Wordsworth (1770–1850).
  • The scene is London at dawn, viewed from Westminster Bridge.
  • The poem is a 14-line Petrarchan sonnet.
  • The city appears beautiful because the morning is clear and unusually quiet.
  • Personification, imagery, simile, metaphor, and contrast strengthen the description.

Think and answer

  1. Why does the speaker say that a person who ignores the view would be “dull of soul”?
  2. What does the comparison “like a garment” suggest?
  3. How does the poet describe the River Thames?
  4. Explain the metaphor “that mighty heart”.
  5. Why is the city’s silence important to the poem?
Main message: the world can surprise us with beauty—even a great city can seem peaceful, natural, and full of wonder.
